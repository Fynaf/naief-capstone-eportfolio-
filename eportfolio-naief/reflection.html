APPLHSCI 9099 · Interim Reflection · April 2026

Final Critical Self-Reflection

ℹ️

This reflection is written at the midpoint of the capstone experience (April 2026). The project concludes in July 2026, at which point a final reflection will replace this interim version.

What I set out to do in this project was straightforward, at least on paper: design and implement a quality improvement initiative to increase genetic testing uptake in the Glomerulonephritis Clinic at London Health Sciences Centre. The clinical problem was real and documented — nephrologists were not ordering genetic tests at a rate commensurate with the diagnostic value of those tests, and patients with potentially heritable kidney disease were not receiving information that could meaningfully shape their clinical management and family planning decisions.

What I actually did turned out to be considerably more complicated than that, and the distance between those two things is where the most important learning happened.

The first thing I learned is that diagnostic clarity about a problem is not the same as intervention clarity. The non-submission rate and the proportion of physicians who had never ordered were numbers I could point to with confidence. What I could not determine from those numbers alone was whether the barriers were capability-based, structural, or both — and each answer pointed toward a completely different kind of response. Holding that uncertainty without prematurely anchoring was the first genuine test of the critical reasoning skills I had previously treated as largely academic. The baseline survey and direct observations during the November–December 2025 education sessions together pointed toward two distinct barriers operating simultaneously: a capability gap in knowledge and consent, and an administrative barrier in the referral process itself. Separating those two diagnoses was what made the PDSA Cycle 1 design coherent — capability building first, structural redesign second once the provincial infrastructure was in place.

The second thing I learned is that quality improvement in a clinical setting is fundamentally a political project. Not political in the partisan sense, but in the sense that change requires navigating existing relationships, institutional mandates, professional identities, and competing priorities simultaneously. The provincial ordering platform — which Dr. Connaughton confirmed at the midpoint meeting was launching within two weeks of our discussion — crystallised this most sharply. A provincial body had independently determined that the same administrative barrier my project had identified was unsustainable at scale, and had designed a structural solution. This was not a failure of my project. It was confirmation that my diagnostic framing was correct. But integrating that information in real time — adjusting the project narrative without abandoning the work already done, repositioning the local QI contribution within a larger system-level change — was a different kind of reasoning challenge than anything a course assignment had prepared me for.

What surprised me most was how much of the project's eventual shape was determined not by what I designed but by what I chose not to do. The decision to not launch a major education campaign on the administrative process before the provincial platform went live was one of the best strategic calls of the project — and it was a decision not to act. Holding back required more confidence than moving forward would have. That experience changed how I think about leadership: the willingness to pause, to name what you are not doing and why, is as important as any initiative you launch.

The storytelling insight from Dr. Connaughton has stayed with me. She observed — based on her own advocacy experience — that scientific evidence moved nephrologists relatively little, but patient stories drove real attitudinal change. I had spent months building an evidentiary case and had thought carefully about how to present data persuasively. The idea that the most powerful argument might be a family's experience of receiving a genetic diagnosis — not a bar chart — was a genuine reorientation. It has direct implications for how I will approach the Capstone Conference presentation in June 2026.

If I were starting over, I would have built the interprofessional relationships earlier and more deliberately. I developed strong working rapport with Dr. Connaughton and productive engagement with the broader team, but much of that consolidation happened in the second half of the project. The activity log gap — from November 21, 2025 through to April 2026 without formal entries — reflects a pattern I want to break: doing the work before documenting it, rather than alongside it. Good documentation is not bureaucracy; it is the evidence base for your own credibility.

This project has sharpened my professional identity in a specific direction. I came in with a general interest in clinical quality improvement and leave with a clearer sense that my strengths are in the diagnostic and framing phases of QI work — identifying what is actually wrong, building the evidentiary case, and designing the intervention logic — more than in the operational execution phase. That is not a weakness to hide. It is a positioning insight that tells me something about the kinds of roles and collaborations I should be seeking. The work of integrating genomics into routine nephrology care is far from finished. This project is one contribution to a longer arc, and understanding my specific contribution within that arc is itself a form of professional maturity.
