Interprofessional Collaboration

Competency Definition

Ability to work collaboratively and effectively within interprofessional teams in a participatory and coordinated approach to reach mutually agreed decisions around health and social issues. Includes appreciation for the importance of multiple professions in healthcare decision making, collaborative leadership across stakeholder groups, and active engagement with conflict, complexity, and role ambiguity.

Quality improvement in a clinical setting is not a solo act. Almost nothing in this project moved without negotiation across professional roles, and that friction was, on reflection, one of the most instructive parts of the experience.

The GN Clinic at LHSC is an interprofessional environment in the formal sense: nephrologists, nurses, administrative staff, genetics specialists, and trainees all have distinct but overlapping roles in the patient's journey. What I learned quickly is that QI work does not slot cleanly into any one of those roles. As a student operating without a fixed clinical title, I had to earn credibility differently in each relationship. With Dr. Connaughton, the relationship was collegial and evidence-grounded, she pushed back when my framing was incomplete, and the project was stronger for it. With nursing and administrative staff, credibility came from demonstrating that I understood their actual workflows, not just the ideal pathway on a flowchart.

The structured education sessions delivered in November–December 2025 were the most operationally intensive interprofessional engagement of the project. Presenting to a mixed audience of nephrologists, nephrology fellows, and nurses required translating the same clinical content into language that was credible and actionable across different professional roles simultaneously. The direct observation during those sessions, noticing where physician engagement dropped as we moved from clinical reasoning to administrative paperwork, was only possible because I had built enough trust in the room for people to respond candidly rather than performatively. I also attended the provincial Clinical Genetics Community of Practice in March 2026.

The genomic champion model discussed at the midpoint review (April 27, 2026), drawn from Dr. Connaughton's own published work, reinforced the value of embedding a collaborative learning structure within the team rather than relying on individual expertise. A nurse specialist in the PKD clinic has already begun initiating genetic testing independently, which is a working example of how expanding interprofessional roles can carry QI work forward when physicians face structural barriers.

Perhaps the most difficult interprofessional challenge was navigating role boundaries around scope. When Dr. Saxe-Braithwaite raised the possibility of home-based administrative support for form completion, I had to respond constructively to a suggestion that touched on labour relations, clinic administration, and provincial policy, all outside my scope but directly relevant to the problem. The skill was not having answers but knowing how to hold the question.

  • Baseline physician survey (n=18): co-designed with Field Mentor; informed by clinic staff consultations
  • Print package: clinical testing checklist, consent guide, patient brochure, decision flowchart; developed in consultation with clinic team
  • Midpoint review meeting (April 27, 2026): tri-party discussion documented in formal meeting notes

Self-Awareness and Reflexivity

Competency Definition

Self-Awareness and Reflexivity involves the ongoing process of examining one's own assumptions, biases, and positionality as they influence professional practice. It requires the capacity to critically evaluate how personal perspectives shape decision-making, and to revise one's approach in response to new information, feedback, and experience.

This capstone required me to confront and revise my own assumptions on multiple occasions. My original project design assumed that a digital app-based workflow tool would be the primary driver of change. When the app build ran months behind schedule, I had to examine that assumption critically: I had over-indexed on technology as the solution, when the more foundational problem, physician knowledge gaps and administrative workflow friction, existed independently of any workflow tool. The PDSA pivot was not just a logistical adjustment; it was a reflexive act of recognising that my initial framing had been too narrow.

A second moment of reflexivity came when provincial guidance for kidney genetic testing was published after the capstone period had closed, which initially felt destabilising. Reflexive practice required me to examine that reaction: the change alters the ordering pathway, but whether it removes the barrier this project identified is untested. Characterising the new process is the first task of the next cycle, not something to assume.

The most uncomfortable piece of self-awareness came late, in seeing the bias in my own design. I built the survey from barriers already reported in the literature, so it could only count how many clinicians endorsed each one. It never asked what any barrier cost the person performing it. The results came back resembling the literature, and I built the package to match. Administrative burden drew the fewest endorsements of any substantive barrier, and it was the step that stopped the pathway.

What this competency has taught me is that self-awareness is not a character trait but a practice, and like any practice, it degrades when not actively maintained.

  • PDSA Cycle 1 report: showing how feedback and unexpected constraints were incorporated into a revised intervention design
  • Midpoint meeting notes (April 27, 2026): demonstrating real-time reflexive reframing of an unanticipated system-level development
  • Scholarly paper (Alhowaiti, N., Western University, 2025–26): critical appraisal requiring sustained examination of assumptions about evidence quality
03

Critical Reasoning and Creative Problem-Solving

Competency Definition

Critical Reasoning and Creative Problem-Solving involves the capacity to analyse complex, ambiguous, or contested situations using systematic inquiry, evidence, and logical argumentation. It requires the ability to identify underlying assumptions, evaluate competing explanations, synthesise information across sources, and generate innovative responses to problems that do not have straightforward solutions.

The central intellectual challenge of this capstone was diagnostic: why were nephrologists not ordering genetic testing even when it was clinically indicated? The barrier analysis revealed that two distinct constraints were operating simultaneously and required separate responses. The capability barrier: knowledge gaps in eligibility screening, consent, and results handling, was addressable through physician education, independently of any workflow tool. The administrative barrier: a nine-minute, two-form, out-of-province requisition process that could not be billed separately, was a structural problem requiring systemic change. Separating these two diagnoses was what made the staged PDSA structure coherent.

The PDSA Cycle 1 design was a direct product of this reasoning process. Rather than defaulting to the original plan with a substitute technology tool when the app developer withdrew, I reasoned from first principles: what is the minimum viable intervention that addresses the identified capability barriers while deferring the structural ones until the conditions for change are in place? The answer, a targeted educational package and print materials for physicians, was leaner and more causally coherent than the original design.

The creative problem-solving dimension showed up most clearly in the design of the physician materials. The standard QI impulse would have been to reduce friction at the ordering step. But having diagnosed the capability barrier as primary in the short term, I had to design a different kind of intervention, one that built physician confidence in consent conversations and results interpretation, not just ordering mechanics.

What I underestimated going into this project was how much of critical reasoning involves managing the gap between what you can know and what you need to decide. That tension between epistemic humility and decisional confidence is one I will carry into future QI and leadership work.

Evidence Artifacts
  • Capstone final report: full problem analysis, dual-barrier identification, and PDSA Cycle 1 rationale
  • Baseline survey findings (n=18): data-driven identification of capability gaps and non-submission rates with multi-factor causal analysis
  • PDSA Cycle 1 report: documenting the pivot from a technology-dependent solution to a physician capability-building intervention
  • Scholarly paper (Alhowaiti, N., Western University, 2025–26): formal critical appraisal of literature, demonstrating evaluative reasoning applied to evidence quality

Evidence-Based Leadership

Competency Definition

Evidence-Based Leadership involves the capacity to ground decision-making, advocacy, and change efforts in the best available evidence. It requires the ability to critically appraise research, apply findings to real-world health system contexts, navigate uncertainty when evidence is incomplete, and communicate evidence persuasively to stakeholders with varying levels of technical expertise.

This capstone was built on an evidence-first logic from the outset. The initial project design drew on published literature establishing both the clinical utility of genetic testing in nephrology and the documented underutilisation of such testing in practice. The baseline data I generated, confirming that 72% of surveyed physicians had never ordered a genetic test and that the form-completion burden averaged nine minutes per referral, were not just findings to report. They were evidence I produced and interpreted to anchor each major decision in the intervention design.

A critical moment of evidence-based leadership came when I had to make the case for the PDSA Cycle 1 structure. Rather than simply accepting the loss of the app developer as a setback, I used the barrier analysis to reframe the situation: the capability barrier was addressable now; the administrative barrier required structural preconditions that were not yet in place. That case had to be made to mentors and clinic staff, and it had to be grounded in the data rather than in circumstance.

Evidence-based leadership also required communicating findings to audiences with very different relationships to evidence. Dr. Connaughton's observation, that patient stories moved nephrologists more than statistical evidence, was itself an evidence-based insight that reshaped how I think about knowledge translation as a leadership function.

Evidence Artifacts
  • Capstone final report: integrating peer-reviewed literature with locally generated baseline data to justify project design and PDSA Cycle 1
  • Baseline physician survey findings (n=18): primary evidence generated during this placement, used to identify and distinguish barriers
  • Scholarly paper (Alhowaiti, N., Western University, 2025–26): critical appraisal of published evidence and its translation to a clinical implementation context

Systems Thinking and Applied Expertise

Competency Definition

Systems Thinking and Applied Expertise involves the capacity to understand health organisations and clinical practice as complex, interdependent systems, and to apply discipline-specific knowledge in ways that account for those interdependencies. It requires the ability to identify leverage points for change, anticipate unintended consequences, and integrate technical expertise with an understanding of broader organisational, professional, and policy environments.

Systems thinking was embedded in this project from the problem formulation stage. The non-submission rate and the proportion of physicians who had never ordered could not be understood as simply physician behaviour problems or form design problems in isolation. They were expressions of a system-level failure: a mismatch between the complexity of the consent and documentation requirements, the existing clinical workflow, the available infrastructure, and physician preparedness.

The provincial platform announcement crystallised this systems lens most sharply. The platform was not an external disruption to the project but a system-level signal: Ontario had independently determined that the existing two-form process was unsustainable at scale, and had designed a structural solution. Understanding this required situating the project within the broader provincial nephrology and genomics ecosystem, recognising that local QI initiatives and provincial infrastructure development are not competing but complementary responses to the same system failure.

The clinical decision support application represents the most integrated expression of applied systems expertise in this project. Developing the decision logic required translating multi-step clinical reasoning into a form precise enough to drive a software application. That translation process exposed ambiguities in the pathway that no written flowchart had surfaced. The app build was handled by an external developer; the clinical logic was authored by the student.

Thinking in systems also shaped what I chose not to pursue. Recognising which levers are accessible and which are not is itself a systems thinking skill, and one that the scope decisions in this project actively developed.

Evidence Artifacts
  • Capstone final report: Discussion section addresses system-level implications of PDSA Cycle 1 and provincial platform launch
  • Barrier analysis and baseline survey (n=18): mapping interconnected capability and administrative barriers demonstrates applied systems thinking
  • Midpoint meeting notes (April 27, 2026): documenting real-time integration of a system-level development into ongoing project framing
  • Clinical Decision Support App and algorithm flowchart: student-authored clinical logic translated into a functional decision tool
