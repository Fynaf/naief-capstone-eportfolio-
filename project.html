About the Project

Project Overview

This ePortfolio documents my APPLHSCI 9099 Capstone Experience at Western University during the 2025–26 academic year. The initiative was carried out in the nephrology service at London Health Sciences Centre (LHSC), under Dr. Dervla Connaughton as Field Mentor and Dr. Marcy Saxe-Braithwaite as Academic Lead. The Glomerulonephritis (GN) Clinic, staffed by four consultants, served as the pilot site for phased expansion into the general nephrology clinics.

Genetic testing is underused in nephrology. Nephrologists at LHSC already hold the authority to order these tests directly, so the gap is not one of permission. In practice, most patients are referred instead to the Kidney Genetics Clinic, where the routine wait exceeds a year. At LHSC the median interval from CKD diagnosis to genetic assessment is 10.4 years.

The QI Question

How can the general nephrology clinics at London Health Sciences Centre, piloted in the Glomerulonephritis Clinic, improve clinician readiness to identify eligible patients and initiate genetic testing directly, reserving Kidney Genetics Clinic referral for complex cases?

Baseline Findings

A 20-question survey was distributed by email to the wider attending and fellow cohort at LHSC in November and December 2025. Eighteen clinicians responded: 12 attending nephrologists and 6 nephrology fellows.

n = 18 respondents 72.2% had never ordered directly 66.7% unfamiliar with the ordering process 88.2% wanted ordering streamlined

Respondents ranked selecting the appropriate test as the hardest step, and navigating the ordering process second. Half were unsure that direct ordering was even permitted. Around 44% reported limited confidence in consent discussions and in interpreting results. Time constraints and paperwork drew the fewest endorsements of any barrier offered, four of eighteen.

That last figure matters more than its size suggests, and I return to it below.

The pattern held against the international literature. Jayasinghe et al. (2021) surveyed Australian nephrologists and found 68.3% did not know which test to order; the local figure for process unfamiliarity was 66.7%. The barriers reported at LHSC were the barriers already described elsewhere.

The Intervention

The package targeted every barrier the baseline identified. It comprised two structured education sessions delivered in late November and early December 2025, and a clinical decision support toolkit: an eligibility and test-selection app, a workflow flow chart, a clinical checklist, a clinician consent discussion guide, and a patient pre-test brochure. A clinic tracker was built to capture screening and case-level data. An e-consult route, documented on the workflow and the checklist, lets a nephrologist put a question to the kidney genetics service without referring the patient.

Cycle 1 was planned as the first of sequential PDSA cycles toward screening at least 70% of GN clinic patients for eligibility by March 2026, with four outcome measures specified.

PDSA Cycle 1: What Happened

The completed workflow was walked through with a consultant in the GN clinic in March 2026. Eligibility screening with the app, pre-test counselling and consent, and DNA banking in the electronic record drew no objection and were judged workable inside a clinic visit. At the test requisition, the consultant said he would not be able to take the work on: too long, and not adaptable to clinic routine. Ordering through the Out-of-Province Panels pathway required Ministry of Health approval paperwork and out-of-province laboratory coordination, estimated at nine minutes per patient, sitting outside the clinic encounter and not delegable to anyone else.

No direct genetic test order was placed during Cycle 1.

None of the four planned outcome measures could be collected, because each depended on the package being taken up in clinic. At the midpoint review in April 2026 the cycle was reported as a feasibility cycle. No feasibility criterion had been set in advance; feasibility was assessed retrospectively against what the implementation attempt showed. All quantitative findings in this project are baseline. There is no post-intervention dataset.

What Cycle 1 Established

The knowledge and process uncertainties the package addressed are addressable. They are not the limiting factor, and further educational investment alone will not change the order rate while the pathway remains incomplete.

The barrier that stopped the pathway was the one least endorsed at baseline. A multiple-select survey item records how many clinicians report a problem, not what it costs the clinician who carries it, and it cannot capture a barrier respondents have not encountered. Every deliverable in the package removes ambiguity, clinical or logistical. Removing ambiguity cannot remove a task.

Cycle 2 and its measurement plan were not run. The conditions that must be true before it can begin are set out in the handover package, and the first of them is to characterise the current ordering pathway from real orders rather than from its description.

The capstone project closed on May 30, 2026.

Provincial Context

System-Level Development

Ontario has since introduced a provincial genetic testing ordering platform. On the out-of-province send-out path, it removes the Ministry of Health application step, which was the longest single piece of the paperwork. It does not change the test requisition, which carries clinical data, or the LHSC Molecular Genetics requisition, form assembly, faxing, routing and tracking. Requisition friction remains.

What the new route costs in clinic time, whether it can be delegated, and whether it fits the clinic day are all unmeasured. Its description has never been checked against a real order. Characterising it is the first task of Cycle 2, not an assumption Cycle 2 can be built on.
