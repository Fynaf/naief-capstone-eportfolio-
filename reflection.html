APPLHSCI 9099 · Final Critical Self-Reflection · July 2026

Final Critical Self-Reflection

My Most Significant Learning

Genetic testing is underused in nephrology. Most patients referred to the genetics clinic wait more than a year, even though nephrologists can order the tests themselves.

I started by identifying barriers, reviewing the literature, and surveying the department to see whether they existed locally. I assumed they all ended the same way, with no test ordered, so I did not ask what each barrier cost. The survey mostly confirmed the ambiguity I had already expected, both clinical and logistical. As a result, I built an intervention package to remove unknowns. I did not notice that I had fallen for confirmation bias, which kept me from reframing the question and tailoring the project design.

When the package was ready, I walked a consultant through it. He could see the concept, but he still could not take on the requisition work, even after we had simplified it. I later checked with the other GN staff, who said the same. Unveiling the ambiguities did not address that specific barrier. It made it visible.

I expected Cycle 1 to work as a pilot and read the absence of orders as failure. What I had not understood was that feasibility questions apply to quality improvement too, asking whether an intervention can be implemented at all. Judged as a pilot, Cycle 1 failed. Judged as feasibility work, it returned a finding.

Collaborative Problem-Solving with Mentors and Colleagues

My reading changed at the midpoint review, working that difference out with my mentors. Dr. Saxe-Braithwaite kept the method realistic, as always, and suggested carrying possible solutions forward as recommendations. Dr. Connaughton offered models that work inside a clinic day rather than alongside it. That collaboration within my own field felt routine rather than a skill. I only appreciated it after collaborating outside medicine on a clinical problem.

We contracted a development team and supplied a written specification, but we never read it together or agreed who would test. When early builds missed my meaning, my instinct was to absorb the work rather than reopen the arrangement. I wrote the logic with my GN co-fellow Antonia Zhu and kept the reviewing internal. It felt efficient, and it spared me the harder conversation. Now I would settle roles and testing responsibility in writing before the build starts.

Critique of My Capstone: Strengths and Areas for Development

My main critique is the early design: I underestimated the implementation risks, and my learning plan was over-ambitious, reduced from five outcomes to four on Dr. Saxe-Braithwaite’s advice.

The strongest part of the project was the eligibility algorithm, which held up against real cases. The other gain was less visible: keeping a weekly log let me read the year as one line rather than separate tasks.

Applying This in the Next Phase of My Career

If I redesigned the project, I would measure what each step costs the person doing it before deciding what to build. I will now use feasibility assessment by design rather than by chance. Clinically, I leave more confident using genetic testing in my own practice.

Growth Across the Key Competencies

The competencies grew out of the same events. Critical reasoning developed in the eligibility algorithm, where I had to settle borderline cases clinicians usually decide by judgment. Interprofessional collaboration developed with the development team, where the gap was less in the specification than in how we had agreed to work. Evidence-based leadership showed up in synthesising the cycle into an honest feasibility report. Self-awareness came late and uncomfortably, in seeing the bias in my own design. Systems thinking changed most: I learned to see the constraint in the system, not just the barrier in front of me.
