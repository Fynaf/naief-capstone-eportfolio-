Final Critical Self-Reflection

My Most Significant Learning

Genetic testing is underused in nephrology even though clinicians can order it themselves. I began this project assuming that once the main barriers were identified, each could be addressed with the right intervention. The needs assessment (n = 18) and the literature pointed to uncertainty about eligibility and counselling, a complicated workflow, and administrative friction. We built a toolkit to address each. It closed the knowledge and confidence gaps, but not ordering. The requisitions and MOH application took about 9 minutes per patient and could not be delegated. No orders were placed in Cycle 1. A cycle that shows an intervention is not yet feasible is not a failed one, and the redesign belongs in the ordering process, not more education.

Collaborative Problem-Solving with Mentors and Colleagues

Dr. Connaughton, my field mentor and provincial lead for kidney genetics, kept the clinical direction sound; Dr. Saxe-Braithwaite kept the project methodologically realistic. Midway through, the three of us agreed to present the work as a completed feasibility cycle and defer outcome measurement. On my own I would have pushed for outcome data and read the stall as failure. The clinical team showed me what the survey could not: after the education sessions they identified ordering itself as the remaining barrier. That became the main finding.

Critique of My Capstone: Strengths and Areas for Development

Two things I would defend. Converting the eligibility guideline into an app algorithm was the most technically demanding work of the capstone, and it held up on real cases. The harder part was interpretive: I had to resist presenting a null result as success and let Cycle 1 stand as evidence for redesign instead. What I would do differently is measurement. The survey identified the barriers, administrative friction among them, but it captured how many clinicians endorsed each barrier rather than what each cost them in time. Knowledge gaps were endorsed most often, so I weighted the toolkit toward them, and the barrier that proved binding looked minor in the data. Now I would measure task time directly. I also planned too much: the first learning plan carried five outcomes and more deliverables than the timeline allowed, and I cut it to four on Dr. Saxe-Braithwaite's advice.

Applying This in the Next Phase of My Career

What stays with me is a sequence rather than a tool: assess feasibility and map the real workflow, including what each step costs the person performing it, before committing to a rollout. Having built this with mentor support, I am confident using genetic testing in my own practice as decision support, with the final judgment mine.

Growth Across the Key Competencies

My clearest growth was in Systems Thinking and Applied Expertise. I started by addressing barriers one at a time. Mapping the workflow taught me how those tasks depended on one another, and that strengthening one part of the pathway has little impact if another step remains the limiting factor. Critical Reasoning and Creative Problem-Solving grew twice: when the eligibility algorithm forced me to resolve borderline cases clinicians usually settle by judgment, and when ordering stalled and I separated physician-dependent from system-dependent steps. Evidence-Based Leadership meant building the case for change from the literature and a barrier analysis rather than my own impression. Self-Awareness and Reflexivity grew when I caught myself designing around the technical idea and losing sight of the competencies it should demonstrate. Interprofessional Collaboration developed differently than I expected: I came in to design and present the intervention, but the plan shifted each time I brought findings back to my mentors and the team.
